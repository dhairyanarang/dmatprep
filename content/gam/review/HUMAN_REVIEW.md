<a id="gam-computational-sciences-cache-policy-001-h1"></a>**[h1]** **A cache and its replacement policy**

<a id="gam-computational-sciences-cache-policy-001-p1"></a>**[p1]** A cache keeps a small number of records close at hand so that they need not be fetched from the slow store each time. When a record is asked for and is already in the cache the request is a hit; otherwise it is a miss, the record is fetched and placed in the cache, and if the cache is full one record must be dropped to make room. The share of requests that are hits is the cache’s hit rate.

<a id="gam-computational-sciences-cache-policy-001-p2"></a>**[p2]** This system drops whichever record has gone longest without being asked for. A hit counts as an asking, so a record that is found in the cache is thereby moved to the back of the queue for eviction; a record that is merely written back to the slow store is not, because writing is not a request for the record.

<a id="gam-computational-sciences-cache-policy-001-t1"></a>**[t1]** *Four caches on one machine, as measured over a working day.*

| Cache | Records held | Hit rate |
|---|---|---|
| Index cache | 64 | 97 per cent |
| Document cache | 512 | 61 per cent |
| Thumbnail cache | 2048 | 88 per cent |
| Session cache | 128 | 79 per cent |

<a id="gam-computational-sciences-cache-policy-001-p3"></a>**[p3]** A hit rate is a property of the pairing of a cache with the pattern of requests it happens to meet. The figures above were measured on one working day’s traffic; a different pattern of requests would give different figures on the same caches, and they are not a ranking of the caches in themselves.

### `gam-cs-cp-001-q1`

> A cache holds three records. It currently holds P, Q and R, which were last asked for in that order, P longest ago. A request arrives for P, and then a request arrives for S. According to the policy, which record is dropped to make room for S?

- **(a)** P
- **(b)** Q ← **KEY**
- **(c)** R
- **(d)** S is added without dropping anything, since the request for P was a hit.

**Why the key is the key.** P was the record that had waited longest, so it is the one an eviction would have taken. The request for P is a hit, and the text is explicit that a hit counts as an asking: P is thereby moved to the back of the queue and becomes the most recently used of the three. That leaves Q and R, and Q was asked for before R. So when S arrives and the cache is full, it is Q that has now gone longest without being asked for.

<a id="gam-economics-port-tariff-001-h1"></a>**[h1]** **A port’s tariff for handling cargo**

<a id="gam-economics-port-tariff-001-p1"></a>**[p1]** The port charges a handling fee on cargo it lands. The fee is worked out on the tonnage of the consignment at a rate that depends on the class the cargo falls in, and each class also carries a minimum charge.

<a id="gam-economics-port-tariff-001-t1"></a>**[t1]** *The classes of cargo, with the rate and the minimum for each.*

| Class | Rate per tonne | Class minimum |
|---|---|---|
| General | 4 euros | 200 euros |
| Bulk grain | 2 euros | 300 euros |
| Refrigerated | 9 euros | 450 euros |
| Hazardous | 15 euros | 600 euros |

<a id="gam-economics-port-tariff-001-p2"></a>**[p2]** Where the tonnage produces a fee below the class minimum, the minimum is charged in its place. The minimum is never added to the tonnage fee, and a consignment is never charged both.

<a id="gam-economics-port-tariff-001-p3"></a>**[p3]** A part tonne is rounded up to the next whole tonne before the rate is applied, however small the part.

<a id="gam-economics-port-tariff-001-p4"></a>**[p4]** Cargo that is transhipped, meaning that it passes between vessels without leaving the port area, is not handled by the port and pays nothing at all. Its class makes no difference to that, and neither does its tonnage.

<a id="gam-mathematics-two-stage-inspection-001-h1"></a>**[h1]** **A two-stage inspection**

<a id="gam-mathematics-two-stage-inspection-001-p1"></a>**[p1]** Every item made on a line goes through a first inspection, and a proportion of items is flagged there for a closer look. This proportion is the line’s flag rate. Only flagged items go on to the second inspection, where some are rejected and the rest are returned to stock. The share of all the line’s items that end up rejected is therefore the flag rate applied first and the rejection rate applied to what it leaves.

<a id="gam-mathematics-two-stage-inspection-001-p2"></a>**[p2]** The rejection rate is quoted as a proportion of the items that reach the second inspection, and not as a proportion of everything the line makes. A line with a high rejection rate may reject very few items overall if it flags almost nothing at the first inspection, and this is the commonest misreading of the table below.

<a id="gam-mathematics-two-stage-inspection-001-t1"></a>**[t1]** *Flag and rejection rates for four production lines.*

| Line | Flag rate | Rejection rate |
|---|---|---|
| Line A | 12 per cent | 25 per cent |
| Line B | 8 per cent | 15 per cent |
| Line C | 20 per cent | 5 per cent |
| Line D | 5 per cent | 40 per cent |

<a id="gam-mathematics-two-stage-inspection-001-p3"></a>**[p3]** Both rates are properties of the line as it is currently set up. Neither moves with the number of items put through it, so a line that runs for longer rejects more items but does not reject a larger share of them.

<a id="gam-mathematics-two-stage-inspection-001-p4"></a>**[p4]** The rates in the table were measured on the tooling in use at the time. A change of tooling alters what the first inspection catches, and the figures must then be measured again rather than adjusted or carried across.

<a id="gam-social-sciences-coding-agreement-001-h1"></a>**[h1]** **Coding open answers, and checking the coders**

<a id="gam-social-sciences-coding-agreement-001-p1"></a>**[p1]** Answers written in a respondent’s own words are turned into categories before they can be counted. The list of categories, with a written rule for what belongs in each, is the coding frame. A share of the answers is double-coded, meaning that two coders work on the same answers without seeing each other’s decisions, and the proportion of those answers they place in the same category is the pair’s agreement rate.

<a id="gam-social-sciences-coding-agreement-001-t1"></a>**[t1]** *Agreement rates for four pairs of coders, on the same set of double-coded answers.*

| Pair | Answers double-coded | Agreement rate |
|---|---|---|
| Pair 1 | 300 | 91 per cent |
| Pair 2 | 300 | 74 per cent |
| Pair 3 | 300 | 88 per cent |
| Pair 4 | 300 | 83 per cent |

<a id="gam-social-sciences-coding-agreement-001-p2"></a>**[p2]** Where an answer could be placed in more than one category, the frame directs the coder to the category named earliest in the list, and to record the answer as contested. Contested answers are counted in the category they were given and are also set aside for the frame to be revised at the end of the round.

<a id="gam-social-sciences-coding-agreement-001-p3"></a>**[p3]** An agreement rate measures whether two coders read the frame the same way. It says nothing about whether the frame captures what the respondents meant: two coders can apply a poorly drawn category with perfect consistency, and their agreement will be as high as if the category were a good one.

<a id="gam-social-sciences-coding-agreement-001-p4"></a>**[p4]** A coder who has seen the other coder’s decisions is no longer coding independently, and the pair’s figure is not an agreement rate at all. The one condition the procedure rests on is that the two work unaware of each other.
